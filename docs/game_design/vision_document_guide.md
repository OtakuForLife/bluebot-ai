# Game Vision Document Guide

## Purpose

A game vision document is the foundational document that defines the core concept, direction, and goals of a game project. It serves as the north star for all development decisions and helps align the team around a shared understanding of what the game should be.

## When to Create

- **Timing**: Immediately after project inception, before detailed design or development begins
- **Audience**: Development team, stakeholders, and decision-makers
- **Lifespan**: Living document that may evolve but should remain stable once approved

## Key Components

### 1. Executive Summary
A concise overview (2-3 paragraphs) that captures:
- The core game concept and hook
- What makes this game unique and compelling
- Target audience and market positioning
- High-level scope and ambition

**Best Practice**: Write this last, after completing other sections, to ensure it accurately reflects the full vision.

### 2. Game Overview
Fundamental details about the game:
- **Genre**: Primary and secondary genres (e.g., "Action-Adventure with RPG elements")
- **Platform**: Target platforms (PC, console, mobile, web)
- **Art Style**: Visual direction (realistic, stylized, pixel art, etc.)
- **Core Gameplay Loop**: The fundamental cycle of player actions (in 3-5 sentences)
- **Session Length**: Expected play session duration

**Example Core Loop**: "Players explore procedurally generated dungeons, defeat enemies to collect loot, return to town to upgrade equipment, then venture into deeper dungeons with increased difficulty."

### 3. Vision Statement
The emotional and experiential goals:
- **Player Experience**: What should players feel while playing?
- **Emotional Impact**: What emotions should the game evoke?
- **Memorable Moments**: What should players remember after playing?
- **Core Fantasy**: What fantasy or aspiration does the game fulfill?

**Best Practice**: Use evocative language that inspires the team. Focus on feelings, not features.

### 4. Unique Selling Points (USPs)
What differentiates this game from competitors:
- List 3-5 distinctive features or qualities
- Explain why each matters to players
- Compare to similar games (what you do differently/better)
- Identify your competitive advantage

**Best Practice**: Be specific and honest. Avoid generic claims like "fun gameplay" or "great graphics."

### 5. Target Audience
Who will play this game:
- **Demographics**: Age range, gaming experience level, platform preferences
- **Psychographics**: Player motivations (achievement, exploration, social, etc.)
- **Player Archetypes**: Bartle taxonomy (Achievers, Explorers, Socializers, Killers)
- **Accessibility**: Considerations for different ability levels
- **Market Size**: Estimated audience size and market opportunity

**Best Practice**: Be realistic about your audience. A niche audience is fine if you understand and serve them well.

### 6. Scope and Constraints
Realistic boundaries for the project:
- **Development Timeline**: Rough phases (prototype, alpha, beta, release)
- **Team Size**: Expected team composition and roles
- **Technical Requirements**: Engine, tools, platforms, performance targets
- **Budget Considerations**: Resource constraints and priorities
- **Risk Factors**: Known challenges or uncertainties

**Best Practice**: Be honest about limitations. Better to scope down and deliver quality than over-promise.

### 7. Success Criteria
How you'll measure success:
- **Must-Have Features**: Core features required for launch
- **Quality Benchmarks**: Standards for polish, performance, stability
- **Player Metrics**: Engagement, retention, satisfaction goals
- **Business Goals**: Sales targets, user acquisition, monetization (if applicable)
- **Creative Goals**: Awards, critical reception, community impact

**Best Practice**: Make criteria measurable and achievable. Include both quantitative and qualitative metrics.

## Writing Guidelines

### Clarity and Conciseness
- Use clear, direct language
- Avoid jargon unless necessary
- Keep sentences short and focused
- Use bullet points for lists

### Specificity
- Provide concrete examples
- Use numbers and metrics where possible
- Reference specific games for comparison
- Describe mechanics, don't just name them

### Inspiration
- Paint a vivid picture of the game
- Use evocative language for the vision statement
- Include mood references (games, films, art)
- Make the team excited to build this

### Realism
- Be honest about scope and constraints
- Acknowledge risks and challenges
- Set achievable goals
- Consider team capabilities

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Too Vague**: "A fun action game" tells us nothing
2. **Too Detailed**: Vision ≠ Design Document (save details for later)
3. **Feature Creep**: Listing every possible feature dilutes the vision
4. **Ignoring Constraints**: Unrealistic scope leads to failure
5. **No Differentiation**: "Like Game X but better" isn't a vision
6. **Missing the "Why"**: Explain why features matter to players

## Format and Structure

- **Length**: 3-8 pages (longer for complex projects)
- **Format**: Markdown or PDF for easy sharing
- **Visuals**: Include mood boards, reference images, concept art if available
- **Versioning**: Track changes and maintain version history
- **Approval**: Get stakeholder sign-off before proceeding

## After the Vision

Once the vision is approved:
1. **Lock it down**: Resist major changes without good reason
2. **Reference it often**: Use it to guide design decisions
3. **Share it widely**: Ensure all team members understand it
4. **Measure against it**: Check if development aligns with vision
5. **Evolve carefully**: Update only when necessary, with team consensus

## Examples of Strong Vision Statements

**Example 1 - Puzzle Game**:
"Players should feel the satisfaction of solving a complex puzzle through careful observation and logical deduction, followed by the delight of watching their solution elegantly unfold. Each level should feel like a 'eureka moment' waiting to happen."

**Example 2 - Action Game**:
"We want players to feel like unstoppable action heroes, chaining together spectacular moves in a flow state. Combat should be rhythmic and dance-like, where mastery feels earned but accessible."

**Example 3 - Narrative Game**:
"Players should feel emotionally invested in characters' fates, making difficult choices that have meaningful consequences. The game should linger in their thoughts long after the credits roll."
